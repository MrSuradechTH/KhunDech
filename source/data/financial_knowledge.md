### Learning Note: Calculating WACC for SET-Listed Companies

WACC represents the blended cost of a company's total capital structure (Equity + Debt). #### 1. The WACC Formula
**WACC = (E/V × Ke) + (D/V × Kd × (1 - T))**

*   **E:** Market Value of Equity (Market Cap).
*   **D:** Market Value of Debt (Total Interest-Bearing Debt).
*   **V:** Total Value (E + D).
*   **Ke:** Cost of Equity (calculated via CAPM).
*   **Kd:** Cost of Debt.
*   **T:** Corporate Tax Rate (Standard Thai rate is 20%).

#### 2. Key Adjustments for the SET Context
*   **Kd (Cost of Debt):** Do not use the coupon rate on bonds. Use the **effective interest rate**, calculated as: *Interest Expense / Total Interest-Bearing Debt*. This reflects the actual interest burden the company faces given its credit rating.
*   **Market Values:** Always use **Market Capitalization** for equity, not Book Value. If the company is thinly traded, consider a 3-year average market cap.
*   **Tax Shield:** The `(1 - T)` component reflects the benefit of interest deductibility. Note: If a company is reporting losses and pays no tax, the tax shield is ineffective; set **T = 0**.

#### 3. Practical Caveats
*   **Debt Definition:** Only include interest-bearing debt (e.g., bank loans, debentures). Exclude trade payables or accruals, as these are "free" operating liabilities.
*   **WACC vs. ROIC:** The acid test for a quality company is **Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) > WACC**. If ROIC < WACC, the company is destroying value regardless of its profitability, as it costs more to fund the operations than the operations are generating in returns.
*   **Volatility:** In the Thai market, interest rate environments shift with BOT (Bank of Thailand) policies. If Thai government bond yields rise, your WACC will rise, which mechanically lowers the "Fair Value" of the company’s future cash flows. Always stress-test your WACC with +0.5% or +1.0% interest rate hikes.

*   **The "Illusion of Profitability":** A company might show high accounting-based EVA because its old, cheap-to-build assets are still generating revenue. However, if the company had to replace those assets at current market prices (via the PPI adjustment), the ROIC would drop, potentially turning a positive accounting EVA into a negative economic one.
*   **Investment Discipline:** This calculation reveals whether management is actually growing the business or merely "milking" depreciated assets. If a company shows positive accounting EVA but negative economic EVA, it suggests the firm is value-destructive if forced to expand at current price levels.
*   **Competitive Moat Accuracy:** A company that maintains a high ROIC even after adjusting for replacement costs possesses a powerful **pricing power moat**. It means they can generate returns that exceed the cost of capital even when their assets are valued at current inflation-adjusted prices.

#### 3. Practical Caveat
*   **Asset Age Profile:** The discrepancy between "Traditional" and "Replacement" EVA is widest for companies with long-lived assets (e.g., utilities, industrial manufacturers, or power plants) purchased during low-inflation years. 
*   **Conclusion:** If you find that a SET-listed company’s EVA shrinks significantly under the replacement cost method, treat their reported "Efficiency Ratios" with skepticism. They are likely benefiting from "vintage asset tailwinds" rather than structural competitive advantages. Always prioritize the **Replacement EVA** when forecasting long-term sustainability.

### Learning Note: Adjusting Invested Capital for the "CapEx Cliff"

To avoid being blindsided by a sudden spike in investment needs, use these two metrics to gauge how much of the company's asset base is nearing the end of its life.

#### 1. The Metrics
*   **CapEx / Depreciation Ratio:** 
    *   **Ratio ≈ 1.0:** The company is reinvesting just enough to maintain existing capacity (steady state).
    *   **Ratio < 1.0 (sustained):** The company is "milking" assets. This lowers Invested Capital (denominator) and inflates ROIC, but creates a **CapEx Cliff** where a massive replacement cycle is inevitable.
    *   **Ratio > 1.5:** Indicates aggressive growth or a backlog of deferred maintenance being addressed.

*   **Accumulated Depreciation / Gross PPE:** 
    *   This measures the "age" of the asset base. 
    *   **High Ratio (e.g., > 60-70%):** Assets are near the end of their useful life. The current accounting book value (Net PPE) is artificially low. 

#### 2. Practical Adjustment to Invested Capital
To reflect the true "CapEx Cliff" in your valuation model:

1.  **Identify the "Hidden" Liability:** If the company has a high "Accumulated Depreciation/Gross PPE" ratio but a low "CapEx/Depreciation" ratio, it is under-investing. 
2.  **Estimate the Adjustment:** Calculate the gap between historical maintenance CapEx and the projected replacement cost of the aged assets. 
3.  **Adjust the Denominator:** Increase your Invested Capital base by the **Estimated Replacement Shortfall**. 
    *   *Formula:* `Adjusted Invested Capital = Current Net PPE + (Accumulated Depreciation × % of assets needing replacement over the next 3-5 years)`.

#### 3. Caveats
*   **Technology Shifts:** Don't assume all assets will be replaced like-for-like. If the company is shifting to automation or green energy, the replacement cost may be significantly higher than the original cost adjusted for inflation.
*   **Asset Utilization:** A high depreciation ratio doesn't always equal a cliff if the company has excess capacity. Check the **Capacity Utilization Rate** (if disclosed in the 56-1 One Report) to see if the assets actually *need* replacing to sustain current output.

**Bottom line:** If a company shows high ROIC but is consistently under-spending on maintenance (CapEx/Depreciation < 1.0) while its assets are physically old (high Acc. Dep.), your valuation must include a substantial "catch-up" CapEx outflow to avoid overstating future Free Cash Flows.

### Learning Note: Incorporating "Replacement Shortfall" into DCF Terminal Projections

A "Replacement Shortfall" occurs when a company's historical CapEx is significantly lower than the true depreciation of its economic productive capacity. If not addressed, your Terminal Value will be artificially inflated by unsustainable cash flows.

#### 1. The Adjustment Logic
You must "normalize" the Terminal Year Free Cash Flow (FCFF) by ensuring that **Maintenance CapEx** in the terminal period equals the **inflation-adjusted depreciation** of the replacement cost of assets, rather than the lower accounting depreciation.

#### 2. Practical Steps for your DCF Model:
*   **Step 1: Calculate the Normalized Maintenance CapEx:** 
    Use the formula: 
    *Normalized Maint. CapEx = (Annual Depreciation Expense) × (Replacement Cost Index / Historical Cost Index)*. 
    *If you don't have a specific index, use the current-year PPI or a rolling 5-year average inflation rate for the sector.*
*   **Step 2: Compare to the Terminal Year Forecast:** 
    If your model’s projected terminal year CapEx is lower than this "Normalized Maint. CapEx," you have a shortfall. You must override your terminal year CapEx to match this higher normalized figure.
*   **Step 3: Adjust the Reinvestment Rate:** 
    The Reinvestment Rate used in your terminal value growth formula ($g / ROIC$) must be updated:
    *New Reinvestment Rate = (Normalized Maint. CapEx + Growth CapEx) / NOPAT*.
    This usually lowers your terminal cash flow, resulting in a more conservative and realistic valuation.

#### 3. Why this matters for the "CapEx Cliff"
If a company has been "milking" its assets (CapEx/Depreciation < 1.0), the market price often reflects current high cash flows. By forcing the terminal year to reflect the **Replacement Shortfall**, you prevent the DCF from "blindly" assuming the company can operate indefinitely on aging equipment without incurring the massive future cash outflows required to stay competitive.

**Caveat:** If the company is in a secular decline (i.e., industry sunsetting), do not assume full replacement. In this case, acknowledge the decline in NOPAT alongside the CapEx drop to reflect a "harvest" strategy rather than a growth one.
